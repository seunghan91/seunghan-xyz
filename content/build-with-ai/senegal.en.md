## What the data says about Senegal

The figure that should rearrange your assumptions is 34.91%. That is the share of sampled conversations from Senegal that look like coursework or learning — more than double the global 16.45%. At the same time, only 36.99% of conversations are work-shaped, well under the global 43.36%. Read those two together and a picture forms that no rich-country/poor-country ranking predicts: the usage index here is 0.41, ranked 92 of 121, yet the conversations themselves skew harder toward studying and skill-building than almost anywhere in the dataset. A guess, hedged as one: the tool is being used less as a shortcut for tasks already assigned and more as something to learn with directly.

What makes that reading sturdier is where Senegal lands against Korea, a country whose usage index of 3.78 is nine times higher. Education and learning conversations run 25.33% in Senegal versus 11.21% in Korea — 126% higher. Software development conversations are 12.14% against Korea's 10.42%. Content creation and copywriting edges ahead too, 27.77% to 27.3%. Senegal leads the far wealthier country in all three, and in education it leads by a factor of more than two. Meanwhile research and intelligence queries sit at 5.21%, barely half the global 10.94% — the pattern is making and learning, not looking things up.

The automation split adds one more texture. 51.82% of Senegal's conversations lean toward handing a task over entirely, slightly above the global 48.62%, and document processing and extraction runs 48% above the global share at 6.4%. Office and administrative support tasks come in at 9.1% against a global 7.89%. That combination — heavy learning share, above-average task delegation, light research — suggests conversations where the tool is already inside a real routine, being taught and being trusted with whole chunks of work, rather than being poked at out of curiosity.
