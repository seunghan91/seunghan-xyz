In United States, 13.59% of sampled conversations were matched to hobbies & lifestyle tasks. In Korea that figure is 6.44%.

## What the data says about United States

The number most likely to surprise an American reader is not a big one — it's a small one. Conversations that look like coursework or learning make up just 8.94% of the US sample, barely over half the global share of 16.45%. Education and learning topics overall sit at 9.28%, roughly 30% below the global 13.23%. Whatever those conversations are — and the data says nothing about who is having them — the American slice of AI talk is distinctly less classroom-shaped than the world average. One plausible reading is that usage there has diffused outward into ordinary life rather than concentrating in study settings; another is that learning-flavoured questions get asked through other channels. The snapshot can't settle that, and it says nothing about change over time.

What fills the space instead is telling. Hobbies and lifestyle conversations account for 13.59% of the US sample, 43% above the global 9.49% — and here the United States plainly leads Korea, 13.59% to 6.44%, a 111% higher share. Research and intelligence tasks also run hot at 13.46% against a global 10.94%. Even software development conversations, at 8.13%, sit well below the global 11.51% — a counterintuitive result for the country where much of this tooling is built, and a decent reminder that the loudest use case online is not necessarily the median one.

Work conversations are 41.32% of the sample, slightly under the global 43.36%, and within them one occupational category punches above its weight: conversations matched to sales and related tasks are 11.6%, about 27% above the global 9.14%. Content creation and copywriting remains the single largest topic at 19.99%, though below the global 22.72%. And the automation-versus-augmentation split is almost a coin flip — 49.59% to 50.41% — meaning conversations there hand tasks to the model about as often as they collaborate with it, right around the global pattern.

The usage index lands at 3.87, ranked 12 of 121, a notch ahead of Korea's 3.78 — two countries at comparable intensity, using the tools in noticeably different proportions. That's the backdrop for Seunghan's offer: free, informal engineering help, peer to peer, from Seoul — a time zone offset that usually works in your favour, since a question sent in the American evening gets answered while you sleep. If your project involves shipping apps to the stores, building AI agents or MCP tooling, wiring up backends and auth, mobile development, or making systems reliable under real load, he's glad to dig in.
