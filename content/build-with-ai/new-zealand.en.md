## What the data says about New Zealand

New Zealand's Anthropic Usage Index is 4.84 — fifth out of 121 countries. That is the number that breaks the usual mental map. Korea, which outsiders tend to picture as a default technology leader, records 3.78, and New Zealand sits comfortably above it. On a per-person basis, few places anywhere lean on these tools as heavily. Part of that plausibly reflects common-knowledge context: an English-speaking country, a small open economy, and a population spread across islands and time zones, long accustomed to using software to shrink distance. But the index alone only says usage is intense; the topic mix says what that intensity is for.

The clearest signal is learning. Coursework-style conversations make up 19.57% of New Zealand's total against a global 16.45%, while work sits at 43.16%, essentially the global 43.36%. One caution before reading too much into that: these are conversations matched to task categories, not descriptions of who is having them. A conversation that looks like coursework does not tell us anything about the person typing it. What it does tell us is that a noticeably larger slice of New Zealand's usage takes the shape of structured learning and inquiry — and the topic breakdown agrees, with Education & Learning at 15.22% of sampled conversations versus 13.23% globally and Research & Intelligence at 11.7% versus 10.94%.

The Korea comparison makes this concrete. Education & Learning conversations run 35.8% higher than Korea's share — 15.22% against 11.21% — and Hobbies & Lifestyle runs 45.5% higher, at 9.37% versus 6.44%. Where New Zealand trails is just as distinctive: only 9.32% of conversations match software development tasks, a fifth below the global 11.51%. Content Creation & Copywriting tops the table at 23.26%, right on the global norm.

Even the automation split tilts the same way. New Zealand's conversations are 53.67% augmentation-leaning versus 46.33% automation-leaning — slightly more collaborative than the global 48.62% automation share. Heavy usage, learning-shaped, hands-on, and comparatively light on software engineering: that combination describes a lot of projects that work beautifully as prototypes and still need someone to make them durable.
