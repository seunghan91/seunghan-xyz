In Croatia, 11.68% of sampled conversations were matched to hobbies & lifestyle tasks. In Korea that figure is 6.44%.

## What the data says about Croatia

If you sorted countries by how intensively they use AI assistants, Croatia would sit in the middle of the table — a usage index of 1.8, rank 36 of 121, well under Korea's 3.78. But sort them by what the conversations are about, and the ordering flips. Conversations matched to hobbies and lifestyle tasks take up 11.68% of the Croatian sample, compared with 9.49% globally and only 6.44% in Korea — an 81.4% larger share than in a country whose overall usage index is more than double Croatia's. Volume and appetite are clearly different things. One reasonable guess is that in Croatia these tools are being woven into personal projects and off-hours tinkering as much as into office work, though the data itself cannot confirm the motive, only the mix.

The same pattern holds in a more technical corner of the data. Conversations matched to software development tasks make up 13.22% of Croatia's sample, above the global 11.51% and 26.9% above Korea's 10.42%. On both measures, Croatia is ahead of Korea — not behind, not closing any gap, simply ahead in what proportion of its AI conversations look like building software. Meanwhile content creation and copywriting runs below the global share (19.47% versus 22.72%), and conversations resembling coursework are also lower than average (13.53% versus 16.45%), with education and learning topics similarly under at 10.82% versus 13.23%. The Croatian profile, in short, tilts toward making things and away from assignment-shaped or copy-shaped usage.

Other splits sit close to the global centre. Work conversations are 43.57% against a global 43.36%, and the balance between handing tasks to the model and working alongside it is nearly even: 49.03% automation-leaning, 50.97% augmentation-leaning. Sales-related job-task conversations run above the global norm (10.61% versus 9.14%), while arts, design, entertainment, sports, and media run below it (11.18% versus 13.55%). These are conversation patterns, not headcounts — the snapshot says nothing about who the users are, what they do for a living, or where any of this is heading, and it should not be stretched into a labour-market story.
