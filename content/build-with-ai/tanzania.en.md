In Tanzania, 22.26% of sampled conversations were matched to education & learning tasks. In Korea that figure is 11.21%.

## What the data says about Tanzania

Fifteen point zero one percent. That is the share of sampled Claude conversations from Tanzania matched to software development tasks — higher than the global 11.51%, and a full 44% higher than Korea's 10.42%. Korea's overall usage index is 3.78; Tanzania's is 0.07, the lowest of the 121 countries measured, rank 121 of 121. A simple ordering of rich country and poor country would predict the opposite of what the data shows. Per conversation, Tanzanian usage skews more toward writing code than usage from one of the most wired countries on earth.

The learning numbers push the same direction. Education & Learning makes up 22.26% of conversations, against 13.23% globally and 11.21% in Korea — nearly double Korea's share, a 98.6% lead. Conversations that look like coursework account for 30.18% of the Tanzanian sample, compared with 16.45% worldwide. The data cannot say who is on the other end of these conversations, and I won't guess at identities. What it can say is that the exchanges themselves are dense with study and construction, and that 44.55% of them are work-related, slightly above the global 43.36%. One plausible reading, offered as a guess rather than a finding: where fewer people use the tool, those who do tend to use it with intent.

The quieter details fill out the picture. Document processing and extraction runs at 6.07%, about 40% above the global share, and conversations matched to office and administrative support tasks run 35.7% above it. Research and intelligence, by contrast, sits at 6.03% — 44.9% below the global 10.94% — and conversations matched to sales tasks run under the global share too. Tanzanian conversations also lean toward automation, 54.86% versus a global 48.62%: more often asking the tool to take the whole task than to assist with it. The overall shape is hands-on — content, learning, code, documents — rather than exploratory.

Two cautions before anyone reads too much into this. These figures describe conversations matched to job tasks, not people's jobs, and this is a single snapshot with no trend line behind it, so nothing here supports a claim about direction or momentum. It is one still frame, and a revealing one.
