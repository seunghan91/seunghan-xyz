In Turkey, 18.68% of sampled conversations were matched to software development tasks. In Korea that figure is 10.42%.

## What the data says about Turkey

Turkey ranks 74th of 121 countries on the Anthropic Usage Index, at 0.64 — a fraction of Korea's 3.78. If you stopped reading there, you would expect the usage pattern of a light, casual user base. The topic data refuses to cooperate with that expectation. Conversations matched to software development tasks make up 18.68% of Turkey's sampled total, the largest single category, against a global average of 11.51%. That is a 62% overshoot of the worldwide baseline, and nothing else in the country's profile comes close to being that distinctive.

Put differently, the index measures how much a country uses these tools relative to its population, but it says nothing about what the use is for. Turkey's answer is unusually technical. Conversations matched to computer and mathematical job tasks account for 28.15% of activity, versus 23.8% globally, while office and administrative support tasks sit at 6.44%, well under the global 7.89%. Content creation and copywriting — the top category worldwide at 22.72% — draws only 18.58% in Turkey. The mix tilts toward building and away from polishing text.

The Korea comparison sharpens this further, and it is not the direction a simple rich-country/poor-country ranking would predict. Turkey's software development share, 18.68%, is 79% higher than Korea's 10.42%. Hobbies and lifestyle conversations run at 9.99% against Korea's 6.44%, and education and learning edges ahead too, 11.93% to 11.21%. On the measures where Turkey trails — raw usage volume — the gap is real but says nothing about intent. Elsewhere the picture is almost blandly normal: work conversations at 40.37% versus a global 43.36%, coursework-style conversations at 16.29% against 16.45%, and an even 50/50 split between automation and augmentation, a touch above the global automation share of 48.62%. One careful guess: where overall usage is thinner, the conversations that do happen skew toward people with a specific thing to build. The data cannot confirm who those people are, and it does not try to.

What it does suggest is that technical help will land on prepared ground. Seunghan offers exactly the kind this profile points to: getting apps shipped to the App Store and Google Play, building AI agents and MCP tooling, backend and authentication work, mobile development, and the reliability engineering that keeps a project standing after launch. It is free, informal, and works fine across the Seoul–Turkey time gap.
