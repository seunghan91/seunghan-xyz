## What the data says about Norway

The most interesting number in Norway's data is not the usage ranking — it's what the heavy usage is *not* about. Norway scores 4.04 on the Anthropic Usage Index, placing 7th out of 121 countries and ahead of Korea's 3.78, yet only 31.17% of sampled conversations were work-related, far below the global 43.36%. High engagement with low work content is an unusual combination; the intuitive assumption that rich countries use these tools mainly to accelerate office output doesn't hold here. A reasonable guess — and it is a guess, since the data describes conversations rather than people — is that in a country with short commutes, long winter evenings, and a culture that takes leisure seriously, much of the usage happens off the clock.

What fills that space is learning. Conversations that looked like coursework accounted for 26.38% of the sample, against 16.45% globally, and Education & Learning was the single largest request topic at 20.1% — over half again the global 13.23%. Conversations matched to educational instruction and library job tasks ran at 17.96%, about 40% above the global rate. Norway also leads Korea here by a wide margin: 20.1% versus 11.21%, roughly 79% higher.

The other topic where Norway clearly outpaces Korea is hobbies and lifestyle: 12.25% of conversations versus Korea's 6.44%, about 90% higher. Meanwhile conversations matched to software development tasks sit at 8.88%, noticeably below the global 11.51%, and office and administrative support tasks are underrepresented too. The interaction style fits the same pattern: only 41.26% of conversations leaned toward automation, compared to 48.62% globally, so a clear majority — 58.74% — stayed collaborative, with the human iterating rather than handing the task over.

Read together, the numbers sketch a country that treats these models less as a workhorse and more as a study companion and a tool for personal projects.
