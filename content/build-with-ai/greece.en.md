In Greece, 10.36% of sampled conversations were matched to hobbies & lifestyle tasks. In Korea that figure is 6.44%.

## What the data says about Greece

A simple rich-country/poor-country ranking would put Greece in the middle of the pack and leave it there — rank 43 of 121 on the Anthropic Usage Index, at 1.51 against Korea's 3.78. The surprise is what happens inside that middling rank. Conversations from Greece matched to software development tasks account for 13.44% of the sample — 16.8% above the global average of 11.51%, and 29% higher than Korea's own 10.42%. By raw volume Greece uses these tools less than Korea; by share of what gets done with them, it builds more. If you assumed the usage-intensity ranking and the build-orientation ranking were the same list, the Greek data corrects you.

The texture of that usage is consistent across measures. Work-related conversations make up 47.13% of the Greek sample versus 43.36% globally, while conversations matched to coursework or learning run at 13.57%, below the global 16.45% — and Education & Learning as a topic sits at 10.65%, Greece's largest deviation from the global pattern at -19.5%. A plausible reading, hedged as a guess: these are conversations happening mid-project rather than mid-syllabus, tools consulted to finish something rather than to understand something. The split between automation and augmentation tells you the tools are being trusted, not just consulted — 47.81% of conversations lean toward handing tasks over, essentially identical to the global 48.62%, with 52.19% leaning toward collaboration. Greece is not an outlier in how it uses AI; it is an outlier in what it points AI at.

One more gap worth naming plainly: conversations matched to hobbies and lifestyle tasks run at 10.36% in Greece, 60.9% higher than Korea's 6.44%. That is the single largest Korea-comparison in the dataset. Paired with content creation and copywriting at 20.52% (just under the global 22.72%) and research and intelligence at 11.44%, the profile is of usage that spans both the workbench and the rest of life — and the caution, as with everything above, is that these figures describe conversations matched to task categories, not the people having them or the jobs they hold.
