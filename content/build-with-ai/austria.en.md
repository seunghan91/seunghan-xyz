In Austria, 11.2% of sampled conversations were matched to hobbies & lifestyle tasks. In Korea that figure is 6.44%.

## What the data says about Austria

The most surprising figure in Austria's data is not how much these tools are used, but how little of that use is work. With an Anthropic Usage Index of 2.61 — 25th of 121 countries — Austria sits well inside the heavy-usage band, yet only 37.2% of sampled conversations matched work tasks, against a global share of 43.36%. Rich countries in this dataset usually tilt toward work-shaped usage; Austria tilts the other way. Nearly a fifth of conversations, 18.17%, matched coursework or learning tasks, above the global 16.45% — though that describes the shape of the conversations, not who was having them.

Where Austria does lean, it leans toward life outside the office. Hobbies & Lifestyle accounts for 11.2% of conversations, the country's most distinctive category at 18% above the global average of 9.49%. Research & Intelligence also runs ahead of the global share, 12% against 10.94%. A plausible reading — and it is a reading, since one snapshot cannot prove motive — is that Austrian usage spreads across curiosity, personal projects, and self-directed learning rather than concentrating in a job description.

The Korea comparison makes this concrete. Korea's overall index is higher at 3.78, yet Austria leads Korea in three categories. Hobbies & Lifestyle conversations are 73.9% more common than in Korea (11.2% versus 6.44%) — the widest gap anywhere in Austria's data. Austria is also ahead on Education & Learning, 12.96% versus Korea's 11.21%, and on conversations matched to software development tasks, 11.07% versus 10.42%. Whatever the index ranking suggests, Austrian usage is broader and, in several lanes, simply more intense.

There is also a difference in how tasks get handed over. Austrian conversations lean toward augmentation 54.06% of the time, with automation at 45.94% — below the global automation share of 48.62%. People there seem more inclined to keep a hand on the wheel, using the model as a collaborator rather than a delegate. That matches the hobbyist-and-researcher flavour of the topic mix: tools used to build and understand, not just to finish.

If that is how you work, the friction points tend to be the same whether you are in Vienna or Graz: getting an app through store review, wiring AI agents and MCP tooling that holds up outside a demo, making backend and auth boring and dependable, shipping and maintaining a mobile app, and reliability engineering that survives real users. Those are exactly the things I help with, free and informally — the time difference between Seoul and Austria is manageable, and the work itself travels fine.
