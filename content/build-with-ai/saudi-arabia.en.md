## What the data says about Saudi Arabia

The number that should stop you is this: in Saudi Arabia's sample, 11.96% of conversations were matched to software development tasks — a higher share than Korea's 10.42%. Korea's overall usage index is 3.78 against Saudi Arabia's 0.93, so on raw volume per person the two countries sit far apart, and yet the Saudi conversations skew *more* technical, not less. The same pattern repeats across the board. Conversations matched to education and learning tasks make up 13.82% of the Saudi sample versus 11.21% in Korea — 23.3% higher — and hobbies and lifestyle comes in at 8.24% versus Korea's 6.44%, a 28% edge. Whatever the overall adoption level, the usage that exists is dense with building and learning.

The work-versus-play split points the same way. 47.26% of Saudi conversations are work-related, against a global average of 43.36%, while coursework-style conversations sit at 16.59%, almost exactly the global 16.45%. So the Saudi profile is not a casual-use profile; it leans toward getting things done. And when people do hand work to the model, the split between automation and augmentation is nearly even — 48.61% automation-leaning against 51.39% augmentation, mirroring the global 48.62%. People there are collaborating with these tools as often as they are delegating to them, which is the same pattern you see in the heaviest-usage countries.

The most distinctive signal in the whole dataset is what the conversations look like at work. Conversations matched to office and administrative support tasks account for 9.96% of the Saudi sample, 26.2% above the global 7.89% — the country's biggest overrepresentation. Meanwhile conversations matched to arts, design, entertainment, sports, and media tasks run at 11.32%, about 16.5% *below* the global 13.55%. Read together with content creation and copywriting holding the top topic slot at 22.94%, a plausible reading is that a large share of Saudi usage is practical throughput: documents, correspondence, operational writing, the unglamorous work that keeps organizations moving. That is a guess, but it is a guess the data supports.

None of this looks like a market waiting for permission. The usage is work-heavy, technically skewed, and in several categories ahead of a country with four times its index.
