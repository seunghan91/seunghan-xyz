## What the data says about South Africa

The number that should rearrange your assumptions is this: South Africa's conversations about education and learning run at 21.32% of the sampled total, against a global average of 13.23% — a 61% overshoot — and against Korea's 11.21%. Korea's overall usage index is 3.78, more than eight times South Africa's 0.46 (rank 86 of 121), yet on this topic South Africa's share sits 90% above Korea's. Intensity and composition are different things, and the composition here is doing something the raw ranking hides.

The coursework signal points the same way. Conversations that look like coursework or learning make up 25.67% of South Africa's total, versus 16.45% globally, and conversations matched to educational instruction and library job tasks run at 15.07% versus 12.79%. Read carefully, this describes the shape of the conversations, not the people having them — the data says nothing about who is typing. What it plausibly reflects is heavy use of these tools as explanation and practice infrastructure: working through material, drafting, questioning, iterating. Meanwhile the categories that shrink are the leisurely ones — hobbies and lifestyle at 6.33% against 9.49% globally, research and intelligence at 8.22% against 10.94%.

The second surprise: conversations matched to software development tasks account for 11.46% in South Africa, essentially the global 11.51% — and slightly ahead of Korea's 10.42%. Work overall tracks the world closely too (44.13% of conversations are work-related, global 43.36%), with content creation and copywriting the largest topic at 23.63%, almost exactly the global 22.72%. South African conversations also tilt toward automation over augmentation, 51.43% to 48.57%, a stronger delegation lean than the global 48.62% — handing over whole tasks rather than only refining drafts. None of this is a profile waiting for permission.
