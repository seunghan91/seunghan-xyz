## What the data says about Nigeria

The most interesting number in Nigeria's data is 38.63%. That is the share of sampled conversations about content creation and copywriting — against a global average of 22.72%, and against 27.3% in Korea, a country whose overall usage index (3.78) is more than fifteen times Nigeria's 0.24. A simple rich-country/poor-country ordering would predict Nigeria trailing on everything. Instead, on the single largest category of use, Nigeria's conversations lead Korea's by 41.5%, and sit 70% above the world average. Low aggregate volume, highly concentrated purpose.

What does that concentration plausibly reflect? One hedged reading: conversations in Nigeria skew toward production over exploration. Research and intelligence make up only 6.73% of conversations, versus 10.94% globally — the country's biggest negative deviation. Meanwhile 47.64% of conversations are work-related (global: 43.36%), and 51.7% lean automation rather than augmentation (global: 48.62%). People appear to arrive with a deliverable in mind and hand the task over. Conversations matched to arts, design, entertainment, sports, and media tasks run at 21.76%, compared to 13.55% globally — and management tasks at 7.17% versus 5.9%. That mix is consistent with a usage profile weighted toward creative output and coordination, though the data describes conversations, not the people having them.

The Korea comparison keeps going. Conversations matched to education and learning: 15.25% in Nigeria, 11.21% in Korea. Conversations matched to software development tasks: 12.97% versus 10.42%. Nigeria's shares are higher in all three of the categories that involve actually making things — writing, learning, building — and coursework-shaped conversations at 17.63% exceed the global 16.45% too. Note what that does and doesn't say: these are conversations that looked like coursework, not evidence about who typed them. The snapshot says nothing about jobs, students, or trends over time; it is one frame, not a film.

So the honest summary is not a deficit story. It is a focus story: fewer conversations per capita, but a larger fraction of them aimed at shipping words, code, and learning.
