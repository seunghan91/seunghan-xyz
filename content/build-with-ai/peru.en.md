In Peru, 27.17% of sampled conversations were matched to education & learning tasks. In Korea that figure is 11.21%.

## What the data says about Peru

Anyone assuming the map of AI usage simply mirrors national income would trip over Peru's first number. Of every hundred sampled conversations from Peru, 41.87 looked like coursework or learning — the global figure is 16.45. In the topic ranking, Education & Learning takes 27.17% of conversations, more than twice the global 13.23%, and on that specific measure Peru runs well ahead of Korea itself: 27.17% against Korea's 11.21%, a lead of 142.4%. Korea uses these tools far more intensively overall — an index of 3.78 next to Peru's 1.05, which places 57th of 121 — but volume and shape are separate questions, and Peru's shape is unmistakably scholastic.

A word of caution about what that shape means. These are conversations matched to task categories, not biographies. A conversation that reads like an exercise or a study session says nothing certain about who typed it, and nothing here identifies anyone as enrolled, employed, or anything else. What can be said is narrower and still interesting: the model in Peru is being used heavily as an explainer and tutor. And the teaching side of the classroom appears too — conversations matched to "Educational Instruction and Library" job tasks run at 17.71% versus 12.79% globally, so explaining flows in both directions.

The supporting numbers sketch a coherent scene. Work-related conversations make up only 36.17% of the Peruvian sample, against 43.36% globally — learning absorbs much of what would elsewhere be office work. Document Processing & Extraction is quietly strong at 7.25% versus 4.32% globally, conversations about wresting structure out of forms and files. Research & Intelligence, by contrast, is subdued at 6.09% against 10.94%. Content Creation & Copywriting holds second place among topics at 26.23%, and conversations matched to software development tasks sit at 10%, a whisker under the global 11.51%. On how tasks are handed over, Peru looks exactly like the rest of the world: 47.58% of conversations lean toward automation against a global 48.62%, with augmentation taking the remainder.

The practical reading: heavy use as a tutor, plus a large writing workload, plus a near-average slice of software conversation, adds up to people one engineering step away from finished products. That step is Seunghan's territory — shepherding apps through store review, building AI agent and MCP tooling, backend and auth work, mobile development, and the reliability engineering that keeps a launched thing alive. A project in Lima or Cusco stuck on any of those is precisely the kind of problem he is glad to pick apart.
