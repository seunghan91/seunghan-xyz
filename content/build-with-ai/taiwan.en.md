## What the data says about Taiwan

Taiwan's Anthropic Usage Index sits at 2.08, ranked 31st of 121 countries — comfortably inside the top quarter, though below Korea's 3.78. The number worth pausing on is not that one. It's this: 14.46% of sampled conversations in Taiwan matched software development tasks, against 10.42% in Korea — roughly 39% higher, and 26% above the global average of 11.51%. On the one task category that corresponds most directly to shipping working code, Taiwan's usage profile leans harder toward building than Korea's does. If you assumed the higher-index country would lead every technical category, the data says otherwise.

The rest of the profile is consistent with that lean. Work-related conversations make up 47.06% of the total, above the global 43.36%, while coursework and learning conversations come in at 12.04%, well under the global 16.45% — and the categories that most under-index against the world are education and learning (9.54% versus 13.23%) and hobbies and lifestyle (6.75% versus 9.49%). Read together, the pattern looks like a usage mix weighted toward applied, output-oriented work: conversations that look like building, writing, and researching rather than studying. Content creation and copywriting leads topic share at 19.78%, with research and intelligence at 12.18%, both close to global norms — so the distinctiveness really does concentrate in the software share, not across the board. The automation-versus-augmentation split (49.66% to 50.34%) sits near the global balance, suggesting conversations divide almost evenly between handing tasks over and working alongside the tool.

One honest caveat: these figures describe what conversations looked like, not who had them. A high software-development share means conversations matched software tasks; it says nothing about anyone's job title. And a single snapshot can't say whether any of this is rising or falling — there is no trend here, only a cross-section.

What it plausibly reflects is a community already deep in the act of making things, which is exactly where Seunghan's help is most useful. If you're in Taiwan building with AI and stuck on the parts between a working demo and a shipped product — getting an app through store review, wiring up AI agents and MCP tooling, designing a backend with auth that won't fall over, mobile development, or the unglamorous reliability work that keeps a service alive — that's what he does, informally and for free.
