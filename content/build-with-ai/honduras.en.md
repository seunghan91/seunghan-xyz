## What the data says about Honduras

The most counterintuitive figure in this data is not the overall usage index — 0.26, rank 105 of 121 — but what sits inside it. Conversations from Honduras matched to software development tasks account for 11.98% of sampled traffic, ahead of Korea's 10.42%, and conversations matched to education and learning tasks reach 16.83% against Korea's 11.21% — a full 50% higher than the country where I live, whose usage index of 3.78 is more than fourteen times Honduras's. A simple ordering by national income would not predict that. It suggests that when people in Honduras open these tools, the sessions skew toward building and learning rather than casual use.

The coursework signal reinforces that reading: 21.92% of conversations look like coursework or learning, against a global share of 16.45%. That describes the shape of the conversations, not who is having them — but combined with a work share of 46.47%, above the global 43.36%, it plausibly reflects a usage pattern weighted toward skill-building and getting concrete tasks finished. Even hobbies and lifestyle conversations, at 8.31%, run 29% above Korea's 6.44%, so the lead over Korea is not confined to one category.

The automation split adds texture. At 52.74%, conversations lean toward handing tasks over to the tool rather than working alongside it, a few points above the global 48.62%. Conversations matched to office and administrative support tasks run 9.64%, well above the global 7.89%, while research and intelligence sits at 7.08%, a third below the global average. My hedge-filled read: the tools are being used as doers — draft this, build this, finish this — more than as research assistants.
